---
title: Management Is the Work of Creating Conditions
description: Lessons about management, productive teams, metrics, change, and the environment people need to do their best work.
---

![Management Is the Work of Creating Conditions](/assets/management-is-creating-conditions.png)

Over the past two years as a manager, I have been trying to understand what good management actually means. I have learned from experience, from other managers, and from books and research on software teams.

Some of the ideas below may sound obvious. Others may be debatable. But together they have changed how I think about the manager's role.

The central idea is simple:

> A manager's job is not to make people work. It is to make it possible for people to work.

That shift sounds small, but it changes almost everything.

### A manager is not simply another member of the team

Many managers were promoted because they were good at the work their team performs. A strong engineer becomes an engineering manager. A skilled analyst becomes an analytics manager. The new role looks like a continuation of the old one, but it is not.

A manager works closely with the team, but is no longer just another peer. The manager has authority, evaluates performance, influences compensation, and makes decisions that affect people's careers. That changes the dynamics of every conversation, even when the manager has good intentions.

This can be difficult to accept. Technical problems are often more comfortable than human ones. Fixing a configuration issue or implementing a feature gives us a clear problem, a set of tools, and a visible result. Understanding why a new colleague is unhappy after three months is much harder.

The temptation is to return to the work we already know. But when a manager spends most of the day solving technical problems, the actual management work remains undone.

The role is different now. The manager needs to remove obstacles, create clarity, protect focus, build trust, and help the team become stronger than any one individual.

#### There is no shortcut to productivity

Software managers often fall for the same set of hopes:

- A new tool will dramatically improve productivity.
- A new programming language will solve the problem.
- Other managers have discovered some secret way to get much better results.
- If we are behind, we must immediately double our output.
- People will perform better if we push them harder.
- Technology changes so quickly that nobody can keep up.
- Everything should be automated, including the human parts of work.

These ideas are attractive because they turn a complex people problem into a simpler technical or procedural problem. Sometimes a better tool really does help. Automation can remove repetitive work. A new process can solve a specific bottleneck. But none of these things can replace a healthy working environment.

Productivity is not something a manager can install.

#### Protecting people's time is a form of respect

One of the worst things a manager can do is waste people's time.

Late meetings, unnecessary meetings, constant interruptions, premature announcements, and frequently changing priorities all break focus. Each interruption may look small from the manager's perspective, but the accumulated cost can be enormous.

Consider an early announcement about a possible reorganization or a future project. The manager may believe they are being transparent. The team, however, has too little information to act and enough information to worry. People spend time speculating instead of working.

Or consider a priority that changes three times in a week. Every change requires people to stop, rebuild context, and decide what no longer matters. Even if each new direction is reasonable, the pattern creates uncertainty.

Good management protects people's ability to think. That means limiting unnecessary meetings, reducing interruptions, making priorities explicit, and avoiding half-formed announcements that create anxiety without enabling action.

#### Pressure wins battles and loses wars

Under pressure, people usually work faster - not longer, and not better.

For a short period, urgency can increase visible output. But the hidden costs appear later:

- Product quality suffers.
- Design work is skipped.
- Technical debt grows.
- People become less proud of what they deliver.
- Job satisfaction declines.
- Burnout becomes more likely.

There are real emergencies, and teams sometimes need to respond quickly. The mistake is turning emergency mode into the normal operating model.

Short-term pressure may move a deadline. It does not create sustainable productivity.

#### The environment matters more than most productivity tricks

Differences in individual productivity can be large, but the exact ratio is less important than the lesson behind it: people do not perform in isolation. Their environment strongly affects what they can accomplish.

Managers often focus on things that are easy to count or change: years of experience, programming language, defect totals, salary, or the number of completed issues. These factors can matter, but they do not explain the whole picture.

The conditions around the work often matter more:

- A quiet and comfortable place to work
- Teammates who help one another
- Enough uninterrupted time to reach a state of flow
- Pride in the product
- A sense of progress and purpose
- The freedom to choose the best way to solve a problem

The discussion after my presentation made this very concrete. One colleague described going home because a busy office had become too noisy. In a peaceful environment, he accomplished more in one day than during the previous two days in the office.

This is not an argument for one universal workplace policy. Some people work best in an office; others need a quieter setting. Neurodiverse colleagues may be especially affected by noise and other sensory input. The management lesson is to pay attention to the environment and give people reasonable ways to do focused work.

A talented colleague can also improve the productivity of everyone around them. They share context, unblock decisions, raise standards, and help others learn. Their value cannot be understood only by counting their individual output.

#### Methodology should be a tool, not a cage

Standard methods can be useful. They preserve knowledge, create a shared language, and prevent teams from repeating avoidable mistakes.

But when a methodology becomes too strict, it can send a dangerous message:

> Management does not trust people to think.

At that point, the method stops supporting the work and starts replacing judgment. People complete forms, attend ceremonies, and follow procedures even when those activities no longer help them deliver anything valuable.

The extreme version is "work to rule": people follow every procedure so literally that progress almost stops. The rules have not changed, but the willingness to apply experience and initiative has disappeared.

Agile methods are useful precisely because they are meant to support adaptation. When the ceremony becomes more important than the outcome, we have missed the point.

A healthy methodology provides guardrails. It should make good decisions easier without pretending that every situation is the same.

#### Metrics should support improvement, not punishment

Metrics are not bad. Measuring a system can help us understand it and improve it. The danger begins when a measurement becomes a threat, a burden, or a substitute for judgment.

If engineers are measured by lines of code, they can write more code without creating more value. If a team is expected to complete exactly 50 story points every sprint, it will become very good at reporting 50 story points. If the goal is to reduce a backlog by 20 percent, the team may optimize the number while leaving the underlying problem untouched.

The system learns to produce the measurement.

This does not mean that goals should be vague or that "do a good job" is enough. A useful objective should explain:

1. What outcome we want
2. Why it matters
3. How we will recognize progress
4. Which quality or safety constraints must not be sacrificed
5. When we will review what we have learned

Numbers can be part of that conversation, but they should be indicators, not ceilings. A target should guide attention without encouraging people to game a proxy.

Measurement works best when the team understands its purpose and helps decide what to measure. The question should be, "How can this information help us improve?" - not, "How can we use this number to control people?"

#### Productive teams beat individual brilliance

A strong team is more than the sum of its members.

People often remember the experience of belonging to a great team long after they forget a particular task or release. Work becomes both more productive and more enjoyable when people trust each other, share context, and take pride in a common result.

That kind of team cannot be created by simply telling people to collaborate. It needs a shared goal that the team understands and accepts as its own.

Leadership can propose a direction, but people do not adopt an organizational goal just because it appears in a slide deck. The team needs to understand why the goal matters and have enough ownership to shape the path toward it.

Once that happens, coordination becomes more natural. People start talking across boundaries, solving dependencies, and making decisions in service of the same outcome.

#### How organizations damage team chemistry

Team chemistry is fragile. Organizations can weaken it through practices that look efficient from a distance:

- **Defensive management:** assigning risk to individuals instead of owning it together
- **Bureaucracy:** making paperwork and approval chains more important than progress
- **Physical or organizational separation:** dividing people who need to solve one problem together
- **Fragmented time:** spreading attention across too many projects and meetings
- **Lower quality standards:** asking people to deliver work they cannot be proud of
- **Foolish deadlines:** committing to dates without the team's input or a realistic understanding of the work
- **Individual competition:** rewarding personal visibility at the expense of cooperation
- **Individual productivity measurement:** turning teammates into competitors

Imagine judging a jazz band by identifying which musician played the most notes. The measurement misses the result. The music only works because the musicians listen to one another.

Software teams are similar. Individual skill matters, but the outcome is collective.

#### Healthy teams need some constructive chaos

A perfectly stable project is not necessarily a healthy one. Projects are living systems. Teams need opportunities to learn, experiment, and renew their energy.

Too much control makes work mechanical. Too much chaos makes it impossible. The useful space lies between them: enough structure to move in one direction and enough freedom to discover a better path.

Managers can create that space through:

- Small pilot projects
- Safe experiments
- Visible wins and moments of closure
- Time to celebrate
- Time to rest after periods of intense work
- New challenges that help the team learn

Change one part of the system at a time. Let the team observe the result and adjust. Sustainable improvement is usually iterative.

#### Change is emotional before it is logical

Managers often explain change with facts, plans, and rational arguments. People may still resist, because the first reaction to change is usually emotional.

Change creates uncertainty. People wonder whether they will succeed, whether their skills will remain valuable, whether their workload will increase, and whether leadership understands the consequences.

This does not make people irrational. It makes them human.

In the discussion after the presentation, one colleague summarized the practical answer well: teams are often comfortable with change once they understand why it is happening. The explanation does not remove every difficulty, but it gives the uncertainty a structure.

For change to succeed:

- Explain the reason clearly and consistently.
- Give people time to adapt.
- Allow a reasonable degree of failure while they learn.
- Listen to concerns without treating every concern as resistance.
- Keep the direction stable long enough for the new way to become familiar.

People need both a compelling destination and psychological room to find the route.

#### The manager's real work

Great management is not pressure, control, or a collection of productivity tricks. It is the deliberate creation of an environment in which people can do their best work.

That environment allows people to:

- Think and focus
- Learn and grow
- Work with pride
- Succeed as a team

A manager cannot remove every constraint or make every day stress-free. But a manager can avoid creating unnecessary stress. They can protect time, clarify priorities, explain change, design useful guardrails, and treat metrics as tools for learning.

The question is not, "How do I make this team work harder?"

The better question is:

> What is preventing this team from doing great work, and what can I do to change those conditions?
